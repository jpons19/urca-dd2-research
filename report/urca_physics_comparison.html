This self-contained comparison concerns the theoretical treatments in the original publications, not particular cooling codes, equations of state, or numerical experiments. “Not included” means that a mechanism lies outside the calculation as formulated, not that it is physically negligible. The detailed reviews cover FM79/Bottaro, Shternin and Alford; Sedrakian has a separate source-grounded comparative note, and Voskresensky diagrammatically; quantitative collective-pion validation remains open.

2. Detailed explanations and source provenance

FM79 — original calculation and β = 0.68

Nuclear interaction. A long-range one-pion-exchange tensor interaction combined with contact/short-range amplitudes described by phenomenological nuclear Landau parameters. In the simplified modified-Urca kinematics the momentum entering the pion factor is approximated by the neutron Fermi momentum: q ≈ pFn, so q²/(q²+mπ²) becomes pFn²/(pFn²+mπ²). This is not the replacement of the pion propagator by 1/pFn.

Short-range correlation factor. FM introduce a short-distance cutoff/correlation prescription applied to pion exchange. The commonly carried-over neutron-branch modified-Urca emissivity reduction is βn≈0.68, for a representative correlation length around 0.7 fm at nuclear saturation density. It is an emissivity-level ratio, not a separate rho-meson-exchange factor and not an NN amplitude multiplier. The specific FM table/page citation from our discussion (Table 1, ApJ p. 552) is retained as a verification item: scan of that exact page has not been re-inspected in preparing this version.

ρ exchange. FM separately examine its influence on bremsstrahlung (not an equivalent complete final OPE+ρ modified-Urca recalculation). Hence neither the β factor nor their rho-suppression estimates should be transferred mechanically to a different complete MU amplitude.

Intermediate propagator. The external-leg weak-emission denominator is simplified by neglecting nucleon recoil/off-shell quasiparticle dispersion, so the amplitude contains a factor of order ±1/ω ≈ ±1/μℓ. This is a separate approximation from setting the momentum transferred through the pion to q≈pFn. Shternin et al., Sec. 2.1, Eq. (5) explicitly identify this denominator as the “traditional” FM approximation. The exact original FM79 formula and printed page remain an editorial item to verify against its scanned article.

Original ADS PDF · DOI. Primary source for correlation construction: Section III; numerical short-range effects: Section V; exchange and rho: Sections VI–VII.

Bottaro et al. — same framework, refined amplitudes

What stays: a nonrelativistic degenerate quasiparticle framework with OPE, Landau parameters and conventional nuclear short-range phenomenology. Bottaro state explicitly that their aim is to revisit FM's calculation and simplify fewer kinematic assumptions, not construct fully screened in-medium nuclear amplitudes.

What changes: explicit ρ-meson exchange, correctly separated spin and tensor terms; full direct, exchange and interference spin/isospin algebra; removal of the triangular approximation to retain proton/lepton Fermi momenta. For triangular neutron-branch MU, the nuclear amplitude separates contact and pion-minus-rho tensor parts, schematically H = 12(f′−g)² + (21/4) h′(pFn)².

β = 0.68: the same historical suppression is applied multiplicatively to the calculated OPE+ρ+Landau emissivity. That particular ratio was not recalculated for this complete interaction. The rho contribution and the phenomenological correlation correction are formally different, but may encode overlapping short-distance effects; one cannot prove numerical double-counting from the factor alone.

Intermediate propagator: not a change from FM. The improved angular phase space and more complete strong matrix element do not restore the energy/momentum dependence of the off-shell nucleon propagators. Bottaro's neutron-branch Eq. (3.22) explicitly gives the lepton-energy factor ωνωℓ/(ων+ωℓ)², later approximating ωℓ≈μℓ. The difference between triangle and nontriangular evaluations is therefore not the Shternin near-on-shell correction. See also Bottaro Appendix A, Eq. (A.4), for amplitudes proportional to 1/ω.

Interpretive caution. A mass change in the pion propagator, medium modifications of the pion–nucleon vertices, and renormalization of the short-range NN interaction are distinct operations. A calculation that varies one while holding the others fixed is a theoretical sensitivity study, not a self-consistent medium-modified-Urca theory.

Bottaro et al., arXiv:2406.18640. Paper: Section 2 (nuclear framework), Section 3.2 (MU); see Eqs. (3.27)–(3.34) for neutron-branch formulas and nontriangular treatment.

Focused comparison — virtual nucleon denominators (FM79 → Bottaro → Shternin)

Two different propagators. The exchanged pion in the strong NN amplitude carries momentum q, with a Born factor (q²+mπ²)−1. The virtual nucleon connecting the strong NN interaction to the weak charged-current vertex has a different propagator GN(E,k)=[E−εN(k)]−1. Approximating q≈pFn (triangle kinematics) is independent of approximating GN.

FM79 / traditional approximation. The nucleon emission diagrams are treated in a soft external-leg approximation, replacing the virtual energy denominators schematically by ±1/ω, where ω=ωℓ+ων≈μℓ. As a source cross-check, Shternin et al. (2018), Sec. 2.1 (discussion following Eq. 5), explicitly attribute GN≈±μℓ−1 to the traditional treatment following FM79. Original FM printed equation/page still needs independent verification.

Bottaro (2024): same weak-emission simplification. Eq. (3.22), printed p. 14 (arXiv PDF p. 15), contains the factor ωνωℓ/(ων+ωℓ)². The authors subsequently use ωℓ≈μℓ for degenerate leptons. The more accurate treatment of strong direct/exchange/interference amplitudes and the removal of the triangular approximation do not eliminate this virtual-nucleon approximation. Appendix A, Eq. (A.4), also shows the 1/ω amplitude structure.

Shternin (2018): restoring the quasiparticle denominator. Their Eq. (4) retains the intermediate proton in diagram A and intermediate neutron in diagram B, and Eq. (5) uses the in-medium GN(E,k). In β equilibrium, the virtual proton in A has EA=μn−μℓ=μp and kA=|pFn−pFℓ| as a vector difference, with magnitude in the interval pFn−pFℓ≤kA≤pFn+pFℓ. Below the direct Urca threshold kA>pFp, but as the threshold is approached the denominator μp−εp(kA) can become small in a restricted angular region. The B-diagram has a corresponding intermediate neutron below its Fermi surface. Their Eqs. (6)–(9) define phase-space averaged enhancement factors using a quasiparticle spectrum; the effect is kinematic and is not pion-softening.

Domains and limitations. Zero-width quasiparticle enhancement increases near the direct-Urca boundary Δpℓ=pFn−pFp−pFℓ→0+. The simplified enhancement factors and perturbative modified-Urca separation cease to be reliable arbitrarily close to or across the threshold. Nucleon widths, finite temperatures and possible diagrammatic overlap require a matched treatment; appending RA,B to a calculation with different NN interaction coefficients is not automatically consistent.

Primary-source map: Bottaro et al., Sec. 3.2.1 Eq. (3.22), Appendix A Eq. (A.4); Shternin et al., Sec. 2.1 Eqs. (4)–(9) and Sec. 3 Eq. (14); FM79 original ADS scan, equation/page of intermediate propagator awaiting direct recheck.

Shternin, Baldo & Haensel (2018) — detailed propagator audit

Main separation. The paper's central new effect is the virtual nucleon denominator, not a resummation of the pion propagator. It also makes an independent, interaction-dependent calculation of strong NN scattering in Brueckner–Hartree–Fock (BHF) theory; these two ingredients must be kept distinct.

Diagrams and propagator (Sec. 2, Fig. 1, Eqs. 4–7)

For the neutron branch the direct amplitude contains two weak-emission orderings. In A the intermediate state is a proton, with Gp(EA,kA); in B it is a neutron, with Gn(EB,kB). The strong matrices are Gpn and Gnn, respectively. Exchange means interchanging identical initial neutrons; those contributions remain present in the full microscopic calculation.

The propagator is GN(E,k)=[E−εN(k)]−1, Eq. (5). In beta equilibrium and the Fermi-surface approximation, EA=μn−μℓ=μp. The usual FM-type simplification replaces the intermediate denominators by ±1/μℓ. Shternin retain momentum dependence and use (Eq. 7) εN(k)−μN=(k²−pFN²)/(2mN*). The effective mass here is the quasiparticle Fermi-surface mass.

Definition of the enhancement (Eqs. 6–9, printed p. 30)

The direct enhancement is the Fermi-surface angular average Rdir=⟨[μℓ/(μN−εN(k))]²⟩. The two diagrams require different momentum intervals:

  • A, virtual proton: RA=2(mp*)² μℓ²/pFℓ · ∫pFn−pFℓpFn+pFℓ dk/(pFp²−k²)².
  • B, virtual neutron: RB=2(mn*)² μℓ²/(pFℓpFp) · ∫pFp−pFℓpFp+pFℓ k dk/(pFn²−k²)². Under charge neutrality pFp≥pFℓ.

Both are pure kinematic propagator averages assuming a weak angular dependence of the strong scattering amplitude; they are not separately measured emissivity ratios of the full microscopic calculation.

For checking the integrals: ∫dk/(a²−k²)² = k/[2a²(a²−k²)] + ln|(a+k)/(a−k)|/(4a³); and ∫k dk/(a²−k²)²=1/[2(a²−k²)]. Thus Eqs. (8)–(9) can be evaluated analytically without an additional nuclear-interaction model.

Near-threshold behavior and interference

Put Δℓ=pFn−pFp−pFℓ>0 below the direct-Urca threshold. For A, its lower momentum endpoint approaches the virtual proton pole from above; for B, its upper endpoint approaches the virtual neutron pole from below. Both direct averages grow as RA,B∝Δℓ−1 at fixed effective masses and Fermi momenta. This is a singular zero-width, leading-order result: at sufficiently small Δℓ, the neutrino energy and finite widths cannot be dropped.

Interference terms between A and B and between direct and exchange amplitudes are included in the microscopic rate, but Shternin argue their enhancement is only logarithmic rather than ∝1/Δℓ, since different denominators approach their poles in different parts of angular phase space. Neglecting interference in the simplified rate is therefore an approximation based on its weaker leading singularity, not an identity that it vanishes.

Strong-interaction model versus practical fit (Secs. 2.2–3, Eqs. 10–14)

The paper uses a nonrelativistic BHF G-matrix from the Bethe–Goldstone equation (10), with the Argonne v18 NN potential and effective Urbana IX three-body force. The radiative amplitude needs a half-on-shell scattering matrix, and its correlation with the near-shell propagator varies over phase space. The microscopic calculation is therefore not simply a fixed interaction multiplied by RA or RB.

The emissivity is normalized by an FM/OPE-style dimensionless nuclear factor αU (Eqs. 12–13). A phenomenological approximation to the authors' full BHF neutron-branch result is αUapprox = 0.6 RA + 0.2 RB (Eq. 14). The numbers 0.6 and 0.2 are not universal angular probabilities: they summarize the nuclear-interaction-dependent overall strength of this fit. Their reported agreement with the full microscopic calculation is model- and range-dependent; it cannot be claimed for arbitrary different NN interactions.

As Fig. 2 demonstrates, the authors compare: an FM/OPE benchmark, a BHF strong amplitude with traditional propagators, a BHF amplitude with in-medium propagators, and Eq. (14). This is the clean conceptual decomposition between NN correlation physics and virtual-nucleon enhancement.

Compatibility with other approaches

With Bottaro: it is meaningful to retain Bottaro's OPE+ρ+Landau angular amplitudes while explicitly reinstating each virtual-nucleon denominator, then redo the full spin/interference angular average. It is not generally justified to take Bottaro's complete emissivity (including its inherited short-range factor) and multiply by αUapprox; that would import BHF-fitted interaction strengths and omit nonfactorizable angular correlations. Multiplying an isolated R-factor can also be insufficient if distinct diagrams are weighted differently.

With Voskresensky: pion polarization/vertex dressing is a distinct correction from virtual-nucleon near-shell propagation. Shternin explicitly warn (Sec. 3, discussion of intermediate pion radiation) that reinstating realistic nucleon propagators could alter the relative importance of pion-line versus external-leg emission; no complete joint calculation is provided in either prescription.

With finite-width approaches: a ∝Δℓ−1 enhancement cannot be used through the direct-Urca threshold. The appropriate comparison is a matched, finite-temperature, collision-broadened charged-current response, rather than adding a divergent MU rate to a separate DU rate. The later Alford–Haber–Zhang NWA and Suleiman–Oertel–Mancini finite-temperature study address related issues with different approximations.

Other explicit omissions: the studied rate is the neutron branch, no self-consistent superfluid response, and quasiparticle residue z is set to unity. Contributions beyond external-leg radiation are discussed but not calculated as a complete in-medium weak-current theory. The soft-bremsstrahlung theorem is not rigorously applicable because the radiated charged lepton carries energy of order μℓ, rather than an asymptotically small soft energy.

Original source: Shternin, Baldo & Haensel (2018), Phys. Lett. B 786, 28–34, especially printed pp. 29–32, Eqs. (4)–(14), Figs. 1–2. Related perspectives: LOCV neutron-branch correlations (2016); LOCV proton-branch correlations (2018); Suleiman, Oertel & Mancini, finite-temperature modified Urca; Alford, Haber & Zhang, nucleon-width approximation. These are distinct calculations, not validation of Eq. (14) for arbitrary interactions.

Alford, Haber & Zhang (2024) — collision widths and direct-Urca crossover

What is fundamentally different?

Unlike FM/Bottaro and Shternin, this is not an explicit five-fermion modified-Urca matrix element with a modified internal-nucleon denominator. Alford et al. reorganize the charged-current polarization tensor (Eq. 3, Figs. 2–3) in terms of dressed neutron and proton propagators and, in principle, a dressed weak vertex. Their leading nucleon-width approximation (NWA) retains the bare charged-current vertex, but adds a constant imaginary Lorentz-scalar mass iWa/2 to each mean-field nucleon propagator (Eq. 6). It is therefore inaccurate to describe this as simply “Shternin + a Γ to remove the pole”.

Origin of the strong interaction: no explicit scattering matrix in the Urca kernel

The chosen widths are Wn=Wp=T²/(5 MeV). As explicitly reported in their Results section, this functional form is drawn from an earlier pure neutron matter Brueckner calculation using the Paris NN potential (their Ref. 27, Eq. 69). Their mean-field matter properties are supplied independently; the width, weak vertex and thermodynamic background are not obtained from one self-consistent scattering matrix. Even for a microscopically computed W, an integrated collision/self-energy strength does not reconstruct the complete momentum-, spin- and isospin-dependent NN amplitudes of Bottaro/Shternin, nor their interference and exchange terms. In their skeleton expansion, missing cross terms reside in vertex corrections (discussion of Fig. 3 and Eq. 3).

The mass-spectral convolution (Eqs. 7–10)

The Lorentz-scalar mass spectral function is Breit–Wigner:

ℛa(m) = [Wa/(2π)] / [(m−Ma*)²+Wa²/4].

They obtain one charged-current reaction rate (their symbol ΓNWA; do not confuse reaction rate Γ with width W):

ΓNWA = ∫dmn dmp ℛn(mn) ℛp(mp) ΓDU(mn,mp).

The direct-Urca kernel is evaluated at the spectral mass parameters while the chemical potentials and other thermodynamic inputs are held fixed (Eq. 10; discussion of Fig. 6). This is a reaction-rate equation. For cooling emissivity one must use the corresponding energy-weighted charged-current kernel; a rate cannot automatically be called an emissivity. The published results compute the full relativistic direct-Urca phase-space integral, not merely an analytic degenerate approximation.

Why is the threshold smooth?

At zero width, degenerate direct Urca requires pFn≤pFp+pFℓ; otherwise the central neutron/proton mass shells are kinematically disallowed. With finite W, Breit–Wigner tails sample shifted mass shells for which this inequality does hold even if the central values are subthreshold. This yields a continuous, nonzero collision-assisted rate. Fig. 6 illustrates the allowed and suppressed regions in the two-mass plane.

Above threshold, the central mass point lies in the allowed region. Since limW→0 ℛ(m)=δ(m−M*), Eq. (10) tends to the ordinary direct-Urca kernel at fixed kinematics away from threshold. Thus the smooth finite-width crossover and narrow-width direct-Urca limit are structural consequences of the approximation, not an independently fitted interpolation curve. A fully evaluated finite-temperature direct-Urca rate is already thermally smeared, so the exact step function is only a strict degenerate-limit idealization.

A pedagogical toy analogue (not their exact two-mass result) is convolution of Θ(−Δ) with a Cauchy-distributed threshold shift of scale w: F(Δ)=1/2−arctan(Δ/w)/π. It displays nonzero subthreshold weight, a smooth transition and the step-function limit as w→0. Do not interpret this toy function as an NWA rate or universal threshold prescription.

Low-temperature powers and the limits of the claimed agreement

Far below threshold and for sufficiently weak broadening, the subthreshold spectral-tail contribution is proportional to W. With W∝T², the ordinary charged-current reaction-rate scaling is T⁷, corresponding to emissivity ∝T⁸; well above threshold the direct-Urca rate scales as T⁵ and emissivity as T⁶. The crossover need not obey either pure power. Fig. 5 directly shows reaction rates, not emissivities.

Alford's Fig. 4 compares the NWA with direct Urca and with an improved explicit OPE modified-Urca calculation using momentum-dependent internal propagators. The agreement in that example is encouraging, but not an interaction-matched proof of the complete MU amplitude: the modified-Urca reference uses OPE, while the NWA width is imported from a Paris-potential study, and interference/vertex terms are omitted. The paper also notes that replacing the full relativistic DU kernel by common low-T matrix-element approximations can change rates by factors of several; this is a model-specific numerical finding, not a universal NWA error estimate.

Vertex and conservation caveats; prospective improvement

A dressed propagator with a bare vertex generally does not reproduce all diagrams implied by the same NN interaction. Alford explicitly warn that width-only dressing violates the conservation constraint for a neutral conserved vector current; for the charged-current isospin-changing case they argue the situation is less restrictive because the current is already nonconserved for differing masses/mean fields. This does not demonstrate that all charged-current vertex corrections are negligible. Their proposed improvements include microscopic widths, additional self-energy Dirac structures, energy/momentum dependence and vertex resummation (Conclusions).

Overall assessment: the NWA provides a genuinely continuous charged-current response covering the DU boundary, but agreement with a complete microscopic modified-Urca calculation below threshold is model- and vertex-dependent. The smoothness claim is stronger than the accuracy claim.

Voskresensky (2001) — MMU diagrams, intermediate-state emission and the closed-diagram formalism

Important hierarchy. The paper's Landau–Migdal medium-modified Urca (MMU) emissivity is a particular interacting nuclear model. Section 4 describes a broader non-equilibrium/optical-theorem (OTF) framework with full in-medium Green functions and closed diagrams. The formalism can encompass quasiparticle explicit-scattering and dressed-spectral calculations, but the explicit MMU expression (25) is not a master formula from which the FM79, Bottaro, Shternin, Alford or Sedrakian calculations follow by changing a single parameter.

These diagrams classify where the radiation comes from, not a unique decomposition into self-energy versus vertex diagrams of a dressed current correlator.

Eqs. (25)–(27): what is new beyond a softened OPE denominator?

The estimated MMU emissivity Eq. (25) contains two collective pion-gap factors (neutral and charged), each in an inverse fourth power, plus a composite correlation/weak-vertex factor Γ⁸ (Eq. 26) and an additional factor F₁ (Eq. 27). Its density dependence and magnitudes therefore cannot be reproduced by simply changing mπ in the single-pion Born tensor amplitude of Bottaro.

The correlation factor Γ⁸ combines Landau–Migdal-type renormalization of pion–nucleon and charged weak vertices. This Γ is not a nucleon collision width, and is distinct from the γN energy-width notation used by Sedrakian or WN used by Alford. Eq. (27) writes F₁ ≈ 1 + [positive intermediate-state correction]; the author explicitly identifies its second term as pion decay from intermediate states, i.e. the first diagram of Eq. (24). Therefore the intermediate-pion mechanism is not just a rescaling of the external-leg diagram. The charged and neutral pion gaps and pairing suppression factors also enter separately.

How does a width-only one-loop calculation relate to these diagrams?

Section 4, Eqs. (42)–(44), printed pp. 26–27: the imaginary part of a dressed boson propagator includes particle–hole cuts that, in the fermion quasiparticle approximation, coincide with squared two-nucleon reaction amplitudes through the optical theorem. Adding such open-reaction rates on top of a response that has already included the same cuts can double-count them.

Eqs. (45)–(48), printed pp. 27–29: the author builds rates from closed nonequilibrium diagrams Π−+, classified by the number of Wightman cuts. In the zero-width quasiparticle limit, cutting the diagrams recovers the explicit one- and two-nucleon reactions. At finite width, there is no unique channel-by-channel decomposition; the dressed one-loop already carries some multi-nucleon collision physics.

Eq. (49) discussion, printed p. 30: the self-energy-dressed one-loop includes a positive squared external-leg amplitude generated by the collision self-energy, whereas other closed diagrams provide interference between emission from different legs and exchange amplitudes. In a fully dressed formulation, collective-pion or particle–hole weak-current contributions also require the corresponding vertices/interaction blocks. It is thus incorrect to equate every Eq. (24) diagram with one unique self-energy or vertex correction: the correspondence depends on how interactions are resummed and how diagrams are cut, and shared cuts cannot be counted twice.

Precise comparison with our other five original papers

  • FM79 / Bottaro: their explicit MU external-leg diagrams correspond most closely to Eq. (24)'s third diagram; short-range cutoff, rho exchange and detailed Born amplitudes are different interaction prescriptions, not automatically recovered from the specific MMU fit.
  • Shternin: improves nearly-on-shell intermediate nucleon propagation while computing a half-on-shell BHF NN scattering matrix. The physical mechanism differs from Voskresensky's additional intermediate-pion and particle–hole weak-current diagrams. Both effects could coexist, but require a matched interaction and amplitude.
  • Alford: a relativistic charged-current bare-vertex, dressed-nucleon one-loop with finite scalar mass widths. Some cut external-leg collision-assisted strength is present; vertex/cross-interference and collective-pion-current completeness are not guaranteed by the adopted widths. Its smooth DU crossover is distinct from the soft-pion MMU density enhancement.
  • Sedrakian: a nonrelativistic bare-vertex one-loop with a broadened proton energy spectral function and sharp neutron; similar incompleteness at the level of matching weak vertices. Correlated high-momentum proton spectral strength need not match Voskresensky's charged/neutral soft-pion polarization.

Conclusion: the nonequilibrium Green-function framework has the widest diagrammatic reach of the approaches compared here, but the published practical MOPE/MMU calculation is a specific model. One should not directly add its Eq. (25) to a nucleon-width charged-current rate or multiply it into another MU formula without a systematic, non-overlapping set of self-energy/vertex/interaction cuts.

Primary source: D. N. Voskresensky, Neutrino Cooling of Neutron Stars. Medium Effects (2001): Fig. 1 printed p. 4; Eq. (24) and Eqs. (25)–(27), printed p. 17; Sec. 4, Eqs. (42)–(49), printed pp. 26–30. Qualitative comparison with later Alford/Sedrakian/Shternin implementations is a diagrammatic interpretation, not a direct result claimed in the 2001 review.

Sedrakian (2024) — SRC-induced proton spectral width and subthreshold Urca

Main organizing principle. Like Alford, Sedrakian computes the weak charged-current polarization as a spectral-function response rather than evaluating an explicit spectator-nucleon modified-Urca amplitude. Unlike the relativistic two-nucleon Lorentz-scalar mass convolution in Alford, the calculation uses a nonrelativistic neutron quasiparticle plus a broadened, energy- and momentum-dependent proton spectral function. The motivation is short-range neutron–proton correlations and the associated proton high-momentum tail. This is not a new fully microscopic NN scattering-matrix calculation.

What is calculated?

Eqs. (5)–(8) express neutrino emissivity through a one-loop polarization containing neutron/proton spectral functions An,p. Eq. (9) uses Aτ(ε,p)=γτ/[(ε−ετ(p))²+γτ²/4], with γτ=−2 Im ΣτR. Eq. (10) isolates the quasiparticle pole. For the actual broadened calculation, An is restricted to that zero-width pole and Ap retains a finite width. In the zero-width limit the usual quasiparticle DU result is recovered (Eqs. 13–15).

The charged-current phase-space factor in Eq. (16) contains 1/2 − (1/π) atan[(εmin−ε)/(γp/2)]: this produces a continuous transition from kinematically suppressed to allowed proton configurations. The arctangent arises from the spectral-function integration, rather than an externally imposed interpolation formula.

How is the width modeled?

Eq. (17): γp(ε,T)=apT²[1+((ε−μp)/(πT))²], i.e. a T² term plus an energy-displacement-squared term. The coefficient ap is estimated from np and pp collision frequencies with assumed angle-averaged NN cross sections; the paper quotes 7 fm² and 4 fm², respectively. These phenomenological collision inputs are not a solution of a Brueckner G-matrix with a specified NN potential and a matched current vertex. One must distinguish a broad high-momentum distribution from a generic non-Fermi-liquid singularity: the stated quadratic near-Fermi-surface damping is also compatible with conventional Fermi-liquid phase space.

Comparison with Alford

Both yield emission below the sharp quasiparticle DU threshold and approach the DU result as spectral widths vanish. Sedrakian: broadened proton only; nonrelativistic energy-spectrum width with both T and ε dependence; explicitly computes neutrino emissivity; SRC/high-momentum-proton interpretation. Alford: both nucleons broadened; relativistic Lorentz-scalar mass convolution; adopted widths proportional to T² from a separate Paris-potential input; primary published observable is a charged-current reaction rate. The two prescriptions cannot be identified by equating their numerical width parameters.

Limitations and interpretation

Figures 1–2 show a smooth emissivity dependence on proton fraction and contrast with quasiparticle threshold behavior. This is a result within the proposed proton spectral model; quantitative dominance over all explicit MU diagrams or conservation-complete response has not been demonstrated from this one-loop width alone. The paper omits muons and superfluidity and does not perform the vertex/self-energy matching needed to reproduce a given OPE, BHF or collective-MMU amplitude. The authors themselves note, in their conclusion, the desirability of calculating the width in a second-Born pion (possibly pion+ρ) model.

Primary source: A. Sedrakian, Short-Range Correlations and Urca Process in Neutron Stars, Phys. Rev. Lett. 133, 171401 (2024), especially Eqs. (5)–(17), Figs. 1–2, and closing discussion.
